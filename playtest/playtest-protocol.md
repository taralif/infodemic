# Infodemic Paper Playtest — Protocol v1
*Companion to playtest-kit.html. Goal: test the core loop AND the one-case-vs-two-cases question. Teens primary, adults welcome.*

## The experiment (v2 — 7/21/2026)

The one-case-vs-multiple question resolved itself: **the feed is the case file.** Every session plays a mixed Feed Drop (16 claims, many subjects, hidden same-story threads). What playtest #1 now tests:

1. **Does the scatter → threads transformation happen?** The board should visibly reorganize as players discover connections. Count threads found, false threads proposed, and whether anyone narrates the transformation unprompted.
2. **Does mixed-subject chaos produce better or worse investigation than a single topic would?** Watch for: subject-hopping that dissipates focus vs. threads that concentrate it.
3. **Can the table handle a LIVE unsettled card?** F·13 has no verdict. Does the group reach "unresolved, and here's what would settle it" — or force a false conclusion?
4. All prior questions: contested-pile energy, persuasion moments, rules-question count, self-run viability.

**What decides it:** Round-2 energy, threads discovered, debrief quality, and who asks to play again.

## Facilitator script (Tara)

- Explain setup once, from the rules sheet, max 3 minutes. Then the only allowed answer to rules questions is: **"What do you think you should do?"** Log every rules question — each is a complexity cost.
- Round 1: secret individual ratings, no discussion, no phones. Poker face at the reveal — your winces are data contamination.
- Round 2: hand over the Three Questions card, say only: "Now you get tools. Phones allowed. Start from the source line." Then sit on your hands. **There is no answer key in play** — the table's evidenced consensus is the standard. Resist every urge to arbitrate.
- Balance: settled-with-case-note claims move toward ACCURATE; unexamined or note-less claims toward FALSE.
- Debrief epilogue: share what fact-checkers eventually found as a *story* ("here's how Snopes traced it — same move you made on card 3"), never as a grade. Where the table's conclusion differs from the record, ask what evidence would change their mind — that conversation is the product.
- **Metric capture (silent):** after the session, score R1 individual slips and R2 consensus calls against the answer keys in /cases yourself. That's the Assembly Code R1→R2 accuracy metric — collected without the players ever being graded.

## Debrief script (~15 min, in order)

1. "Tell me about a moment you changed your mind. What did it?" *(decision-quality probe)*
2. "Which claim fooled you the hardest? What would have caught it?"
3. "If I removed one rule, which would you not miss?" *(the ruthless one)*
4. Session B only: "Was the second case better or worse than the first? Why?"
5. "What's different about how you'd scroll tonight?" *(transfer probe)*
6. Program's three (log answers verbatim): Could you have played this without me? Did the game do what you'd expect it's for? Did it give you something you wanted?

## Co-design upgrade (per Cooney Center toolkit — codesignwithkids.org)

Teens are design partners, not test subjects. Two additions to any session with teen players:

- **Mission from Mars opener (10 min, before setup):** an adult plays a Martian who has never seen a phone: "Explain to me how you decide what to believe on your feed." Teens articulate their actual algorithm folk-models — this is design research on the exact question the game targets, and it warms the room better than rules.
- **Designer debrief add-on (5 min, after the regular debrief):** hand teens the designer's chair — "you've played it; what would you change? what would make your friends want to play?" Log verbatim. Credit contributors by first name in the repo's THANKS file (with parent OK) — being credited is the point for a lot of kids.
